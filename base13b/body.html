3D Squaring Lab

In the plane, squaring a point squares its length and doubles its angle. A point in space has two angles and no agreed way to double them, so every 3D Mandelbrot starts by choosing an approach. Choose one here, give it any equation, and check which properties of the flat set (the ordinary 2D Mandelbrot or Julia set) it keeps. With its dials at their starting values, every approach here except the classic Mandelbulb has exactly the flat set as its slice at z = 0, and the 2D view draws that slice.

With the power between −1 and 1 there is little or nothing to draw. Try a power of 2 or more, or −2 or less.

Kleinian group

A true 3D Kleinian group: three shape-keeping maps of space. A point is written as q = x + yi + zj (a quaternion, a number with more than one imaginary part), and the maps are q → q + 2, q → μ + 1/q and, as the third, q → q + 2L·j. The solid is every sphere those maps can carry two flat walls onto. The picture is turned inside out through one sphere so that it fits in view.

Without the third map the pearls sit along one line. With it they spread in every direction. The quaternion μ sets how far apart the walls are (its i part), how the pearls lean (real part) and how they lean in depth (j part). At a cusp the strings of pearls just touch. Above it they hang apart. Below it they overlap and the picture turns to dust. Each cusp is named by a fraction, the usual way of numbering them. The fraction is a label and sets nothing.

Move the centre of the turning-out sphere and the same group gives a different picture. Keep it in a gap between pearls. On a pearl, the solid fills the whole picture.

A list entry sets the group and its turning-out sphere and nothing else. The camera and the depth of the search stay as you left them. Frame it moves the camera to the view recommended for the chosen entry.

What survives the move to 3D

The figures in the right-hand column are measured from the approach’s own power rule at the current n, by testing how it stretches a tiny circle around each of 6,000 directions.

Why no approach passes every row

In the plane, squaring stretches every direction by the same factor, 2|z|. Doubling the angle stretches the circle of directions by 2, and squaring the length stretches outward by the same amount, so tiny shapes keep their shape. That is the source of the endless clean detail in the flat set.

To copy this in space, a rule would have to stretch the sphere of directions by exactly 2 at every point. A sphere cannot be laid over itself that way: a patch magnified 2× is flatter than the sphere it has to land on. Liouville proved the general statement in 1850: in three dimensions the only shape-keeping maps are built from rotations, scalings and sphere inversions, and squaring is not one of them.

That is also why Kleinian groups look the way a 3D Julia set ought to. They use sphere inversions one after another, the one family the theorem allows, so they keep their detail in every direction. Quaternions (numbers with more than one imaginary part) enter only as bookkeeping: with them 1/q is a sphere inversion that stays inside 3D, where q² only gives the approach called Quaternion square (Lathe).

Sphere inversions and the flat set

Sphere inversions can give the exact flat set at z = 0. What cannot do it is one inversion after another: a chain of inversions keeps every shape, and no power does that in space. Inversions added together can.

In the plane, zⁿ can be written exactly as a sum of circle inversions. Put spheres where the circles were and the same sum gives a power for points in space, and its slice at z = 0 is the flat set. That is the approach called Sphere inversions, and the list has it as the entry Sphere inversions, one plane. Adding is what makes it possible, and adding is also where the perfect shape-keeping is lost.

One formula, nothing placed by hand

Nothing in Sphere inversions, one plane is placed or adjusted by hand. Every point goes through the same formula, the sum of sphere inversions described above. Worked out, that sum multiplies two angles of a direction by n: its longitude, and its latitude as a Mercator map stretches it. That is why the approach was called Mercator before. The two forms were checked against each other for n = 2, 3, 4, 5 and 8, and they agree.

In the list, the entry Sphere inversions, one plane is that formula and nothing more, and the page opens with it. The entry after it, Sphere inversions, two planes, is an earlier version, kept as an entry of its own so that the two can be compared. Its Planes dial sets how many planes through the x-axis carry the flat set. There the count is a free choice that has nothing to do with the power: p planes give the solid 2p fins around the x-axis, whatever n is. The dial goes from 2 up to 8.

Phases that make the solid pulse

The usual Mandelbulb has two phase offsets, one for each angle. Each is added to its angle every time a power is taken. Both Mandelbulbs here have them as dials in the Approach section. Press the play button beside the latitude phase (the θ phase in the classic one) and the solid pulses from its poles. The longitude phase (the φ phase) turns the solid instead.

Sphere inversions, one plane has a matching pair. Turn is its longitude phase, and it turns the solid. Lift is the nearest thing it has to a latitude phase. Lift is an offset where the phase is an angle, so it runs from one end to the other and does not come back round: at power 8 it slides the ring of bulbs up and down the solid. Sphere inversions, two planes has Lift and no Turn, because with two or more planes a turn tears the solid into sheets.

Questions from testing, answered

Why did the Sphere inversions solid come apart when it was sliced away from z = 0?

The version you tested is the one the list now calls Sphere inversions, two planes. It copies the flat rule onto a second plane, at right angles to the first. The rule then jumps across the plane y = 0, and the jump tears the solid unless c lies on the x-axis. On z = 0 nothing of this shows, which is why that slice looked right. The page now opens with Sphere inversions, one plane. That rule has no jump, so nothing is torn. A slice away from z = 0 shows rounded shapes with clean edges. It can still show more than one shape. The Mandelbrot solid and the Douady rabbit came out as one piece at every height that was tried. A string of beads, such as the Julia set for c = −1, is joined only at its necks, which lie close to the plane z = 0. A slice higher up misses the necks and shows the beads as separate discs.

Should the number of planes be forced by the maths?

Yes, and with one plane it is. Think of every direction from the centre as a point on a ball, the sphere of directions. A rule for space moves those points about. Ask three things of the rule: it squares lengths, its slice at z = 0 is the flat set, and it keeps shapes on that ball, so that a tiny circle drawn on the ball is still a circle after the move. One rule is left, with its mirror image, and it is the one the page opens with. The mirror image has an answer of its own further down. That is a short argument of ours, not a published theorem. The rule gives the “inflated” sets of Figure 3 in Kučera’s paper: rounded bodies with no tear at any height, and with detail around one plane only. Where the flat set is a string of beads, so is the body, and a slice above the necks shows the beads as separate discs.

Should the plane come from where the point starts?

That idea is in the list as Quaternion square (Lathe): every point uses the plane through the x-axis and itself. It is forced too, and it is clean. It gives rings where one hopes for beads. The answer on quaternion rules, further down, says more about it.

Where is the look of Figure 4, with beads around two circles?

In Kučera’s paper Figure 4 exists only for c on the real axis, which here is the x-axis. The paper says so: “so far the second construction is unfortunately limited to c ∈ R” (page 444). On this page the same picture appears under Sphere inversions, two planes, with c on the x-axis. The jump across the plane y = 0 is still in the rule. But for such a c the set on one side of that plane is the mirror image of the set on the other side, so the two sides meet without a tear and the jump does not show.

Show Figure 4 goes to the Julia set for c = (−1, 0, 0) at power 2, under Sphere inversions, two planes, with Planes at 2 and Lift at 0, and frames it. It also switches off what would hide that solid or change it: the 2D view, the cut, the modifiers and a trap that is the solid. If one of your zoom limits or your drawing radius would keep the solid from fitting in the picture, it is moved just far enough. Your iterations, quality and colours stay as they are. Back to one plane chooses Sphere inversions, one plane. From a Mandelbrot or Julia picture it changes nothing else. From the Kleinian view it also goes to the Julia set and puts the camera back to its starting view, because an approach shows nothing in a Kleinian picture.

Six new rules were tried, in two rounds, to get that look for every c without a jump: three “pillow” rules, which press the ball of directions flat like a cushion with a front and a back and do the doubling on that flat shape, a smooth blend of the two planes, bulbs about the x-axis, and a pillow rule that keeps tiny circles round. Judged by their pictures, none works. A rule that is exact on two planes at right angles and never jumps has to do one of two things to the ball of directions. It can fold the ball over, so that a patch of it is laid back on top of its neighbour like a crease in cloth. Or it can wrap the ball three times around the spot where the negative x-axis comes out of it, like thread going three times round a finger. The first gives a swollen mass and dust. The second gives rocks and fibres. So a Figure 4 for every c, and with it a Figure 4 Mandelbrot solid, is an open problem.

Is there a solid with no seam that is 3D all the way through?

The nearest thing found is one tick away. Every rule has a mirror twin: take the same step, then swap up and down, so that z becomes −z. Under Modifiers that is the tick box “flip z”. Points of the plane z = 0 do not notice the swap, so the slice at z = 0 is still the flat set. For Quaternion square (Lathe), and for Sphere inversions, one plane with its dials at their starting values (Turn 0, Lift 0), a Julia set whose c lies in that plane does not change at all: the pictures match pixel for pixel. The Mandelbrot solid does change, because there c leaves the plane.

Under Sphere inversions, one plane, the thin lens becomes a tall body, and its slices keep a smoothed outline of the Mandelbrot set up to about half its height, where the slice of the plain rule is already a round disc and soon after that empty. Tick 2D view and move “Height z of the cut or slice” to see them. The plain rule pushes a point away from the plane the same way at every step, so its solid thins to nothing at the edge of the flat set. The twin turns the push round each time, and the solid stands tall. This twin is the mirror image named above: the other rule that squares lengths, keeps the flat slice and keeps tiny circles on the ball of directions round.

The quaternion square is the entry Quaternion square (Lathe). Its twin has the Mandelbrot set in the plane z = 0 and its three-cornered cousin, the tricorn, in the plane y = 0, joined without a seam. The same twin is in the list a second time: at power 2 it is what Quaternion square, twisted gives with “Tilt of the axis, degrees” at 180. The twin of the Mandelbulb is fins and feathers in every direction. None of the three is Figure 4, which wants the same flat set in two planes. Each button shows the Mandelbrot solid at power 2 with the approach’s own dials at their starting values, and switches off what would hide that solid or change it. Like Show Figure 4, it moves a zoom limit or the drawing radius only when one is in the way, and leaves your iterations, quality and colours alone. Back to the plain rule unticks flip z.

Is there a quaternion rule that works everywhere in space?

Yes, one: the quaternion square. It is in the list as Quaternion square (Lathe). Sphere inversions with no plane chosen give exactly that rule: for the square, the sum uses two spheres, centred on the points 1 and −1 of the x-axis, and two points on one line need no plane. The two were checked against each other on 200,000 points. The rule spins the flat set about the x-axis, which gives rings, because squaring a quaternion never leaves the flat plane that holds the x-axis and the point itself.

The entry after it, Quaternion square, twisted, changes one thing. Squaring multiplies a number by itself. Here the second copy is twisted first: a quaternion has two directions outside the flat plane, and in the second copy those two are turned together by twice the tilt. The rings break up into one body with tunnels. The slice at z = 0 stays exact. For whole powers it is the flat set at every tilt, which was checked on 2,000 points at seven tilts for the powers 2, 3, 5, −1 and −2. For a power that is not a whole number the slice differs from the flat set in a wedge of the plane, as wide as the tilt, beside the negative x-axis. Do not expect fine bumps all over the solid: in the close-up pictures its skin is smooth sheets and folds. The edge of the flat set, with all its detail, is there where the solid meets the plane z = 0.

Each button shows the Mandelbrot solid at power 2 with the entry’s own dial at its starting value, “Spin multiplier (odd)” at 1 or “Tilt of the axis, degrees” at 40, and frames the whole solid. Like a twin button, it switches off what would hide that solid or change it, flip z included, moves a zoom limit or the drawing radius only when one is in the way, and leaves your iterations, quality and colours alone.

The tilt is yours to move. At 0 the twisted square is the plain one, and at 180, for an even power, it is its mirror twin. A tilt with a minus sign gives the mirror image of the Mandelbrot solid, with y and −y swapped. At 90 and −90 that solid is one fat body with spikes. It ends at every tilt: nothing further than 2 from the centre belongs to it. One more thing to know: a Julia set whose c lies in the plane z = 0 is a true slice of the set in 4D, where quaternions live. A Mandelbrot solid needs a c that leaves that plane, and adding it as a point of 3D space is this page’s own choice.

Could the hidden rotation axis of the flat picture show the way?

It is a good question. In the flat picture, multiplying turns the plane, and seen from 3D that is a turn about the z-axis. But a turn needs a plane to turn in. The axis is what is left over, and only in 3D is that a line. To carry the x-axis to a point v, the plane to turn in is the one that holds the x-axis and v. Its axis is the z-axis exactly when v lies in the flat plane, and it is another line for every other v. Doing that turn to v itself doubles its angle from the x-axis. Square its length as well, and that is the quaternion square again.

In 4D a turn leaves a whole plane in place, not a line, so “about the w-axis” names the same turn. What 4D adds is a second turn, in the hidden plane, at the same time. The twisted square is what makes that second turn count. Show the twisted quaternion square, in the answer above, puts it on the screen.

Do quaternions or the third map have a part in this?

“Use the third map”, in the Kleinian group section, is the same kind of move as the third map in Kučera’s paper: it is what lets a Kleinian group fill space instead of one plane. It is not the paper’s group, and the squaring rules have no such switch. Quaternions are how the Kleinian maps are written down. Squaring a quaternion is in the list too, as Quaternion square (Lathe) and as Quaternion square, twisted.

Why did the orbit trap only recolour the surface in 3D?

Because the trap was used for colour only. Colour and light now has a drop-down, “In the 3D view the trap”, with three choices: Colours the surface, Is the solid, and Is added to the solid. With Is the solid, the object is made of every point whose orbit comes within the Trap thickness of the trap shape, so each Trap shape gives a different object. This works in Mandelbrot and Julia pictures and in Kleinian pictures. In a Kleinian group that leans (the real part or the j part of μ not 0), or with the trap centre off x = 0 and z = 0, a few balls still come out cut open, with a flat face, and a note beside the choice says so. That part is not cured. The solid keeps its colours at every quality setting. “Orbit steps skipped at the start” means the same in every kind of picture: at 0 the trap shape itself is part of the solid, and at 1, where it starts, the shape is left out and its first echo shows. Higher numbers peel the large outer pieces away. The drop-down Examples, just below, has nine settings that came out well in testing. The 2D view is as it was.

Does the 2D view use a distance estimate, and can the inside have colours of its own?

Yes to both. The outline of the flat picture is drawn from the same distance estimate the 3D rays use, which is why thin threads show as clean lines. With 2D view ticked, Colour and light shows three more controls: “Outline width, pixels”, “Glow beside the outline, pixels” and “Inside the set, colour by”. The inside can stay Plain, or be coloured by Orbit trap, Where the orbit ends up, How deep inside, or How quickly the orbit settles, and “Palette for the inside” gives it colours of its own. In the Kleinian view the last of these shows how deep inside a point is, because that search has nothing that settles. Coloured by the orbit trap in the outside’s own palette, the inside starts half-way round the palette, so that the set does not look like a hole. Until you move these the 2D picture looks as it did. In the 3D view, with the solid cut open, the tick box “Colour the cut face like the inside of the 2D view” does what it says.

Was the glow around the solid with the rough distance estimate a fault?

Yes, and it has been fixed. A ray that ran alongside the solid crept forward in tiny steps, ran out of steps in mid-air, and was then shaded as if it had hit something. Such rays now get past the solid, and a ray that runs out of steps away from the surface shows the background. The new trap solids were checked for it too. Two small things are left with the roughest settings: seen from the side, the outline can differ a little from the exact one, and at Ultra fast with many iterations a bead can show a dark patch. Balanced has neither.

Why did solids look like a stack of slices, even at Fine?

The slices were in the colouring, not in the shape: drawn in one plain colour, the same surface was smooth. A ray stops a hair above the solid, and the colour used to be read from there, from every step of the orbit. The height of that hair changes from pixel to pixel and from one quality setting to the next, and every further step of the orbit turned it into another ring. Now the colour is read from the surface itself, and the trap reads a fixed number of steps: the number on the dial “Orbit steps the trap reads” in Colour and light. So a place keeps its colour at Ultra fast, Fast, Balanced and Fine, whatever “Surface threshold, pixels” is set to, and while you zoom in. Raise the dial for finer patterns. Steps too fine for the picture to show are left out and come in as you zoom in. In the Kleinian view the search now stops at the pearl the ray hit, which keeps its colours steady too. Two things still move a colour a little: Iterations below about 24, where the solid itself is still changing shape, and a Surface threshold of 3 pixels, which draws the solid fatter. The picture is calmer than it was. A Glow width of 2 or 3 brings more colour, and so does a Trap radius of 0.7 in the Mandelbrot picture.

What does the page open with?

With what you asked for: the Balanced quality, Colour by at Orbit trap, the Sphere shell as Trap shape, “How near it came, as a glow”, the Rainbow palette reversed, and the Kleinian Depth of the search at 150, the top of its slider. “Orbit steps the trap reads” starts at 4.

How far can the dials go?

Extreme ranges, at the top of the controls, lets most dials reach five to ten times further: Iterations, Depth of the search, “Ray steps, at most”, Refining passes, Power n and more. A dial with a wider range shows the word wide while the switch is on. Switching it off brings every value back inside its usual limits.

What does a list entry change?

Only what it is about. A Julia entry sets the point c. A Kleinian entry sets the group and its turning-out sphere. The camera, the iterations, the depth of the search, the quality and the approach stay as you left them. Frame it, beside each list, moves the camera to a good view of the chosen entry. As it was found, beside the Julia list, goes back to what the entry was found with: Sphere inversions with one plane or with two planes, and the iterations. It shows only while yours differ, and the words on it say what it will change.

The Julia points are a drop-down now, like the Kleinian list, with your saved points at the top. Six entries look like their names only under Sphere inversions, two planes, and they stand under “Found with two planes”.

Are there Kleinian entries that move the turning-out sphere?

There were none, and that was not intended. The list now has 15 of them under “Turning-out sphere”, and nothing was taken away. They keep the μ of groups that were already in the list and move the sphere. Two of them, the 1/3 cusp entries, also switch the third map off.

How do I get a picture or a 3D model out?

Save a picture draws the view as it is when you press Start the picture, at the Size, Quality and File type you choose, while you carry on. Changes made afterwards do not reach a picture that is already being made. When it is ready, press Save the file.

Save a 3D model turns the solid into a mesh of triangles and packs it in a zip as STL, OBJ and PLY files, which Blender, Rhino and 3D printing programs open. Press Start the model, then Save the file. Its colours are read from the surface the same way the picture’s are; a coarse model shows fewer and broader colour bands than the picture, and a higher resolution brings them back. The first model of a formula takes a few seconds longer while it is prepared, and the progress line says so for as long as it lasts. A solid that is cut open gets the colour of the cut face there. One kind of solid does not come out like its picture: one made of sheets thinner than a cell of the grid, such as a negative power. The panel says so when it happens, and what brings the model closer: untick Keep only the largest piece and choose 256 or 512 cells. Even then the model shows whole outer shells that the picture draws only in part.

One step is untested. On the published page a file reaches you through the page that hosts the lab, which asks you to confirm each file. That could not be tried on the test bench, so the first real test of saving a file from the published page is yours. If a save fails, the lab says so and keeps the file, and you can press Save the file again.

Why does the picture wait after I choose another approach or equation?

Each approach and each equation needs a drawing program of its own, and so do the Kleinian view and a power other than 2. A trap that is the solid needs one too, and so do colours for the inside of the set. The browser takes a moment to prepare a program the first time it is needed, and the picture says “Preparing the formula” meanwhile. After that it is kept, so going back is quick.
