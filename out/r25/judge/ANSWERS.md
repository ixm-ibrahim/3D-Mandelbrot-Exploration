## (1) Did anyone find a rule or an object that is inflated, has bulbs all over by themselves, and is not stretched?

1. No. Not for z^2 + c. (measured, seen)
2. Eight independent starts built some forty rules and objects. Fourteen new rules keep the flat picture exact under my own check (0 wrong of 30,000, nine sets each). None of them gives clean round bodies off the flat picture for both c = -1 and the rabbit. (measured, seen)
3. One object has the whole look: balls on balls on balls all over, perfectly round, nothing lopsided, made of sphere inversions only. It is a group picture with no c and no power, and its flat cut is a chain of circles, not the flat picture. (measured: 39 of every 100 parts of the true c = -1 area get the wrong verdict; the balls are round to 1 to 3 parts in 100)
4. It can never be a rabbit: three round balls cannot meet in one point. (theorem)

## (2) The nearest things, and exactly what each gives up

1. Nearest to the user's picture of "right", for c = -1 only: the rule with bulbs spaced evenly round four special spots (pillow's "skew pillow"). Bodies stay apart; smaller bodies with buds of their own stand on them, off the flat picture, by themselves. (seen, picture 1)
   It gives up:
   - test 3: thorns on the skin and loose crumbs (measured: about a thousand crumbs; one plane has 26);
   - "not stretched": the smaller bodies are eggs, about twice as long as thick (measured 1.9 to 2.4; the buds of two planes and along score 1.4 the same way);
   - evenness: the smaller bodies sit on one side of the flat picture (measured: 18 of every 100 parts of the solid have no partner on the other side), and the solid is not its own mirror image although the flat picture is;
   - "nothing chosen": for the mirror-image rabbit it gives one lump four times the size, not the mirror image. A left or right was built in. One plane, two planes, along and along once all pass this test. (measured, new)
   - test 4 for every other c: the rabbit is rough small lumps with crumbs, c = -0.75 and the Siegel disc are swollen. (seen, picture 2; measured)
   It does not fall apart when c is lifted out of the flat plane. (measured, seen)
2. Nearest for the rabbit: the rule that looks at where the bodies meet (fromc's "twin_tri"). Even, bodies apart, flat picture exact. But it is one plane with a scratched skin: only 3 to 6 parts in 100 of it lie outside the one-plane shape, and those are thorns. Loose flakes float round the rabbit. It has a tear. It crumbles when c is lifted out of the flat plane. Gives up tests 3 and 4. (measured, seen, picture 2)
3. Nearest at another power: bulbs spaced evenly for z^3 + c at c = i (wall's lead). A blown-up body with smaller bodies in many directions; holds at finer blocks and with c lifted. (seen, picture 3; measured)
   It gives up: the square itself; roundness (two of the smaller bodies are round, six are 2.6 to 3.5 times as long as thick, all thorny); evenness (the flat picture is the same after a third of a turn, the solid is not: about 14 parts in 100 have no partner; wall did not test this); and every c with three bodies round a point (one lump).
   I tried the one pattern that would keep the third of a turn (three special spots, power 3; nobody had run it). Flat picture exact. One ridged lump. (measured, seen)

## (3) Does the wall stand? The honest sentence

1. It stands for z^2 + c. Nobody got through, and nobody has a proof that nobody can.
2. Proved: only sphere inversions keep small shapes round in space, and the flat square is not made of them. So every rule with the exact flat picture squeezes something. (theorem, cited)
3. Proved: a second flat plane that also holds the flat picture exists only when c lies on the x-axis. That is why two planes and along work for c = -1 and not for the rabbit, and it is what the paper's author ran into. (theorem)
4. Proved: bulbs with the same spacing all over a ball need two, three or four special spots. Two is "one plane". (theorem)
5. Added this round: with four spots the pattern cannot sit the same above and below for the square (wall, theorem), and cannot repeat three times round for the power 3 (mine, reasoned and measured). With three spots it is lopsided for the square and a lump for the power 3 (measured). So even spacing is lopsided or lumped at every power tried.
6. Added this round: a quaternion carried along the orbit is the quaternion square (rings) when c is on the x-axis, and leaves nothing off the flat picture for the rabbit. Three sections found this independently and their numbers agree. (their measurements; not re-measured by me)
7. Corrections to the briefing: (W-a) holds only for rules that square the length, or that give the plain ball at c = 0 (wall, torus). "The triangle rule fuses c = -1 by its nature" is wrong: turned to face the meeting point it comes apart (fromc; I re-measured: one piece before, separate bodies after). (W-e) names a symptom, not the cause.
8. A published theorem (Martin 2004) says that rules which keep each side of the flat plane to itself must squeeze without bound for every polynomial. I read it only as quoted in a 2025 paper, not in the original. It does not cover rules that fold space onto the flat plane, so it is not the whole wall.

## May be told

- Nobody found it this round, from eight different starting points.
- Bulbs all over, by themselves, perfectly round, with nothing stretched, do exist, built from sphere inversions alone. But that object is a cousin of the flat picture: its flat cut is a chain of circles. It has no c and no rabbit.
- Sphere inversions are quaternion arithmetic, so the user's guess about quaternions is right for that object. A quaternion square or a quaternion carried along the orbit only spins the flat picture round a line.
- With the flat picture kept exact, the nearest shape for c = -1 has smaller bodies off the flat picture, but they are long, thorny and on one side, and the same rule turns the rabbit into rough lumps.
- Bulbs spaced evenly all over a ball need special spots, and with those spots the shape comes out lopsided. That is forced, not a mistake in the build.
- Two planes and along work for c = -1 because c sits on the x-axis; that trick is proved to exist only there.
- "Along once" is still the cleanest rabbit we have.
- It is proved that only sphere inversions keep shapes round in space and that the flat square is not one. It is not proved that the user's object cannot exist.

## May NOT be told

- That the power 3 at c = i is "a clean case" or "a lead". It is lopsided by force, its bodies are long and thorny, and the fix for the lopsidedness is a lump.
- That any new rule "works for c = -1". The nearest one is one-sided, thorny and has a left and right built in.
- That it is impossible. No proof covers every rule.
- That Martin's theorem settles it. I read it second hand, and it covers only rules that keep each side of the flat plane to itself.
- That the pearl tree is a 3D basilica. Its flat cut is wrong for 39 parts in 100 of the area and the eye sees circles where the true picture has points.
- That the lump of the root-set rule ("zip") is confirmed as delivered: its rule returns nothing for c = -1 until a number fault on the upright axis is removed.
- Any internal name, angle or percentage as an explanation.
