Along once, then one plane

The first squaring of every orbit is done by Turning plane, along, and every later one by Sphere inversions, one plane. For c = −1 the middle body keeps its buds in eight directions, and the rabbit comes apart into smooth separate bodies. But the other bodies of c = −1 are bare off the flat plane, two bodies of the rabbit float free, and the Mandelbrot solid is the one-plane solid with thin wings standing off it that have no thickness.

Turning plane, along squares every point about a plane of its own, at every step. That gives c = −1 its buds in eight directions, and it swells the rabbit into one lump. This entry does it once. The first squaring of an orbit is the one of Turning plane, along, and from the second squaring on the orbit follows Sphere inversions, one plane. So a point belongs to a Julia set of this entry exactly when its first step lands in the solid that Sphere inversions, one plane draws for the same c. What was measured in round 21, point by point, and seen in cuts: for c = −1 the middle body has eight buds round it (eight separate stretches of the set on circles round the x-axis through the buds). Close up the eight are not alike. The two in the plane y = 0 carry smaller buds on their sides, as in Turning plane, along. The two in the flat plane are bare on their sides, as in Sphere inversions, one plane. The four at 45° are plain lenses. The rabbit comes apart into smooth bodies, also off the flat plane, where Turning plane, along has one lump. Where it falls short: the bodies to the left and right of the middle body of c = −1 are bare off the flat plane. Cut at 45° and at 90° they show no buds. For c = −0.75 the bodies are fused into one mass. The rabbit is in three pieces: two of its bodies float free, one above and one below the rest. Each holds 7.2% of the volume and stands 0.11 away from the rest, the same on a coarse grid and on a fine one, so the gap is real. For a c out of the plane z = 0 it does no better than Sphere inversions, one plane: with the rabbit’s c turned 10° or more about the x-axis no point of the set was left. The picture does not show that emptiness. It shows jagged shards there, where no point was found that stays (the numbers are in the next paragraph), and shards are one of the things this search is trying to get rid of. The Mandelbrot solid has the body of the solid of Sphere inversions, one plane: of 200,000 points c tested with 80 steps in round 21, none got a different answer. But the picture also shows thin curved wings standing off its left end, where Sphere inversions, one plane has nothing: in one picture 68 of 195 rays stopped on them. The wings are there, but they are films with no thickness. Followed along a line of sight into three of them, the orbit stays longer and longer the nearer the film (18 to 22 steps at the best of 20,000 places along a stretch 0.08 long, 39 to 57 steps when the search is narrowed to a stretch of 0.000000000003), and no place was found that stays for good. And it is not one rule: it is two rules and a count that says when to change from one to the other, which nothing in the flat rule asks for. Powers other than 2 have not been looked at for this entry.

The lab always draws this rule with “Distance estimate” at “Rough”, whatever that setting says. Measured on the Julia sets for c = −1, the rabbit, the Siegel disc and c = −0.75, which all lie in the flat plane, 1,728 lines of sight each, against the set worked point by point: with “Rough” no ray stopped where the set has no body, and of the lines that meet the set at most 4 of 323 were missed. With “Exact” up to 44% of the stops were at nothing, and with “One neighbour” up to 8%. For a c out of the flat plane (the dial “c along z” away from 0) that does not hold, and the picture can show what is not there: with the rabbit’s c turned 10° about the x-axis it shows jagged shards, and with c = (−1, 0, 0.3) two flat flakes, though of 400,000 points tested each time none stayed for the 40 steps the picture asks for. The lab stops a ray at the places where orbits hold out longest, and Sphere inversions, one plane does the same for such a c (it stopped 244 of 1,200 rays for a c whose set is empty). The pictures described here were made with Iterations at 40. What an orbit carries here is a count of its squarings and nothing else, and every orbit starts its count afresh. A squaring of the exact origin does not count. So in a Mandelbrot picture, where the orbit starts at 0, the squaring done by Turning plane, along is the squaring of c. When the equation takes several powers in one step (as in z² + z³ + c), they all belong to that one step: in the first step each of them is taken by Turning plane, along. A power written into the start value (as in the start value c²) is taken before the orbit begins and does not count as a squaring: it is taken by Sphere inversions, one plane. The measurements on this card look at one squaring, so for this entry they show the first squaring of an orbit only, which is the squaring of Turning plane, along. Every later squaring is the one of Sphere inversions, one plane, and the card of that entry describes it. The Learn tab follows the orbit: its first step shows a first squaring, and after “Next step” the moves are those of the squaring the orbit has come to. A product of two different points is taken from Sphere inversions, one plane.

First squaring of an orbit: v² = Rot(ψ)·P(Rot(−ψ)·v) with P the power 2 of Sphere inversions, one plane, Rot(a) the turn about the x-axis by the angle a, φ the angle of v round the x-axis (0 on the side y > 0 of the plane z = 0), and ψ = φ − sin 4φ / 4. ψ is how far the reference plane of v is turned out of the plane z = 0. It stands still where φ is a multiple of 90°. The 4 in it is twice m = 2, the whole number nearest to the power, so it follows the power and is not a free choice. Every later squaring: v² = P(v). The orbit carries a count of its squarings and nothing else.

===== pulledplane | Pulled plane
--- section (as the page shows it):
APPROACH AND EQUATION
APPROACH

Pulled plane

Each orbit carries a plane through the x-axis. It starts as the flat page and is pulled toward c and toward every point of the orbit, and each point is squared by the one-plane rule about it. For c = −1 it gives round buds in four directions, and the rabbit and the Siegel disc are clean separate bodies. But the four directions fade as c leaves the x-axis, the rabbit is nearly the one-plane rabbit, thin loose flakes hover over the skin close up, and three numbers in the rule were set by hand.

Sphere inversions, one plane squares every point about the flat page. Turning plane, along gives every point a plane of its own. Here the plane belongs to the orbit. Think of the planes through the x-axis as the pages of a book whose spine is the x-axis. Every orbit starts with the book open at the flat page, held weakly. Before each squaring two things pull at the page: first c, then the point that is about to be squared. A thing far from the x-axis pulls hard, and a thing on the x-axis does not pull at all. Each pulls toward the page it lies in itself, less directly the nearer that page is to the open one. The pulls add up and none is forgotten, so the page turns less and less as the orbit goes on. Then the point is squared by the one-plane rule about the page as it stands. What was measured in round 21, point by point, and seen in cuts: for c = −1 a row of separate bodies with round buds in four directions, two in the flat plane and two in the plane y = 0 (four separate stretches of the set on circles round the x-axis through the buds, 19° and 15° wide, where Turning plane, along has eight of 13°). The cut along the plane y = 0 looks like the flat picture. The rabbit and the Siegel disc are clean separate bodies in one piece, where Turning plane, along has a lump. Where it falls short: the four directions are there for c on the x-axis and close to it only. For c = −1 + 0.2i nothing is left in the plane y = 0, and for a c a little off the x-axis a ledge runs across the front of every body. The rabbit is nearly the rabbit of Sphere inversions, one plane (one point in eight of its inside differs), so its smaller bodies sit round the flat page only. Close up, thin loose flakes hover over the skin of c = −1 between the two rows of buds: in 336 of 720 directions tested there, and still there after 600 steps, where Sphere inversions, one plane has none. At 45° there is no bud, only a thin loose crescent. The Siegel disc loses 15% of its points between 30 and 200 steps before it settles. For a c that lies in neither of the two planes the set can vanish: with the rabbit’s c turned by 10°, 30° or 45° about the x-axis no point of it was left. The picture does not show that emptiness. For the turn by 10° it shows a smooth thin slab with buds along one edge, and for c = (−1, 0, 0.3) a thin branching thread, where next to no point stays (the numbers are in the next paragraph). The Mandelbrot solid shows the flat set in the plane z = 0 and, but for a few points, in the plane y = 0 (104 of 84,000 places differ), with smooth skin between the two and no buds there. Three numbers of the rule were set by hand, and they matter: see the three dials. Powers other than 2 have not been looked at for this entry.

v² = Rot(θ)·P(Rot(−θ)·v) with P the power 2 of Sphere inversions, one plane, Rot(a) the turn about the x-axis by the angle a, and θ the turn of the plane that the orbit carries. The plane is kept as w = |w|·(cos 2θ, sin 2θ), and a fresh orbit starts with w = (0.05, 0): the flat page, held weakly. Before each squaring w is pulled twice: by c with the weight 16, then by the point v with the weight 1. A pull by a thing with the coordinates (y, z) and the weight k: t = twice the angle of the thing round the x-axis, less 2θ, brought between −180° and 180°; a = 2θ + t − 0.7·sin t; then w becomes w + k·(y² + z²)·(cos a, sin a). The numbers 0.05, 0.7 and 16 are the three dials. They were set by hand.
HOLD OF THE FLAT PAGE AT THE START
±0.01

How firmly an orbit holds the flat page before anything has pulled at it. At 0 the first pull decides the plane alone. The larger the number, the longer the flat page holds, and the nearer the picture comes to Sphere inversions, one plane. Set by hand: 0.05.

HOW LITTLE A THING NEAR THE PAGE TURNS IT
±0.01

How a thing pulls. At 0 it pulls the plane straight toward the plane it lies in itself. At 1 a thing that lies close to the plane pulls toward the plane as it stands, and only a thing that stands square to the plane pulls toward its own. Set by hand: 0.7. Measured for the rabbit in round 21: at 1 a third of the set is lost, at 0.5 the bodies fuse where they meet.

PULL OF C, TIMES
±0.5

c pulls at the plane before every squaring, and this is how many times its pull counts beside the pull of the point. At 0 c does not pull. A c on the x-axis never pulls, whatever this number is. Set by hand: 16. Measured for the rabbit in round 21: at 4 the bodies fuse where they meet.
