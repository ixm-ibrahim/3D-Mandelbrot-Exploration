3D Squaring Lab

In the plane, squaring a point squares its length and doubles its angle. A point in space has two angles and no agreed way to double them, so every 3D Mandelbrot starts by choosing an approach. Choose one here, give it any equation, and check which properties of the flat set it keeps. The first approach is built by adding sphere inversions together, and its slice at z = 0 is exactly the flat set.

APPROACH
Sphere inversions
Add up sphere inversions, the way circle inversions add up to zⁿ in the plane.
v² = |v|² · [1 + 2·inv(Σₖ Rₖ·inv(d − ωₖ))] with d = v/|v| and k = 0 … 1. Before that, the nearest of 2 planes through the x-axis is folded onto the xy-plane.

In the plane, zⁿ is exactly a sum of circle inversions: zⁿ = 1 + n / Σₖ ωₖ/(z − ωₖ), with ωₖ the n-th roots of 1. Swap the circles for spheres and the same sum works on directions in space, where inv(q) = (x, −y, −z)/|q|² is a sphere inversion with a flip and Rₖ turns about the z-axis by the angle of ωₖ. Worked out, it multiplies the longitude and the Mercator latitude by n. It reproduces the Julia sets in Václav Kučera’s paper (Bridges 2019): one plane gives his inflated sets, two planes his truly 3D figure (Julia set at c = (−1, 0, 0), seen straight down).

PLANES THAT CARRY THE FLAT SET

Planes are a free choice, separate from the power: p planes give 2p fins around the x-axis whatever n is, so the fins match the power when p = n/2. Kučera’s 3D figure is 2 planes at n = 2. The flat set’s own (n − 1)-fold symmetry is a turn about the z-axis, a different thing.

With one plane there is also a turn dial. The folds need the flat set’s mirror symmetry, which a turn would break.

Why no approach passes every row

In the plane, squaring stretches every direction by the same factor, 2|z|. Doubling the angle stretches the circle of directions by 2, and squaring the length stretches outward by the same amount, so tiny shapes keep their shape. That is the source of the endless clean detail in the flat set.

To copy this in space, a rule would have to stretch the sphere of directions by exactly 2 at every point. A sphere cannot be laid over itself that way: a patch magnified 2× is flatter than the sphere it has to land on. Liouville proved the general statement in 1850: in three dimensions the only shape-keeping maps are built from rotations, scalings and sphere inversions, and squaring is not one of them.

That is also why Kleinian groups look the way a 3D Julia set ought to. They use sphere inversions one after another, the one family the theorem allows, so they keep their detail in every direction. Quaternions enter as bookkeeping: 1/q is a sphere inversion that stays inside 3D, where q² only gives a lathe.

Sphere inversions and the flat set

An earlier version of this page said that inversions cannot give the exact flat set at z = 0. That was wrong as worded. One inversion after another cannot do it: a chain of inversions keeps every shape, and no power does that in space. Inversions added together can.

In the plane, zⁿ can be written exactly as a sum of circle inversions. Put spheres where the circles were and the same sum gives a power for points in space, and its slice at z = 0 is the flat set. That is the first approach in the list, Sphere inversions. Adding is what makes it possible, and adding is also where the perfect shape-keeping is lost.

Nothing is drawn by eye, and the planes are a free choice

The page used to say this approach was rebuilt by eye. It is a formula, and it is now worked out step by step. The sum of inversions comes to the same thing as multiplying the longitude and the Mercator latitude of a direction by n, which is why the approach used to be called Mercator. The two were checked against each other for n = 2, 3, 4, 5 and 8.

The number of planes has nothing to do with the power. With p planes the solid has 2p fins around the x-axis, whatever n is. So the fins match the power when p is half of n: 4 planes for n = 8, 3 for n = 6, 2 for n = 4. For n = 2 that would be 1 plane, which gives the inflated set. The 3D figure in Kučera’s paper uses 2 planes at n = 2, and that is the starting value here. No value is the correct one. The dial goes up to 8 planes.

Phases that make the solid pulse

The usual Mandelbulb has two phase offsets, one for each angle. Each is added to its angle every time a power is taken. Both Mandelbulbs here have them as dials. Press the play button beside a phase dial to sweep it, and the solid pulses from its poles.

Sphere inversions has the same pair. Lift does what the pole phase does. Turn is the phase of the longitude, and it is offered with one plane only, because with two or more planes a turn tears the solid into sheets.
